# OpenPS usage guide

## 1. Before you start

On the console:

1. Turn it on, or put it in **rest mode** (to wake it from the app, enable _Settings → System → Power Saving → Features Available in Rest Mode → Stay connected to the internet_ and _Enable turning on PS5 from network_).
2. **Settings → System → Remote Play → Enable Remote Play**.
3. Keep the console and your Mac on the same network. Wired Ethernet on the console gives the lowest latency; Wi-Fi 5 GHz is fine.

On the Mac, build and open the app (`scripts/install.sh`, then `open install/OpenPS.app`). The first time macOS may ask to allow local network access: allow it, otherwise the app cannot see the console.

## 2. Pair a console (once per console)

1. **Consoles → Add console** (or **Add your console** on the first-run guide).
2. Choose your console from the list (or type its IP address) and its type, then **Continue**.
3. On the console: **Settings → System → Remote Play → Link Device**. It shows an **8-digit code** that expires after a few minutes.
4. Type the code in the five-box field (pasting works) and **Pair**.
5. **PS5 only:** the app asks you to sign in to PlayStation Network first (the pairing needs your account id). **PS4** needs no sign-in.

A wrong or expired code is reported and you can retry. **Forget** (trash icon) removes the pairing; you need a new code to pair again.

### Registering over the internet through PSN (experimental)

For consoles that refuse the 8-digit code (seen on PS5 system software 14.20) pairing goes through your PSN account instead. OpenPS supports this: **Settings → PlayStation Network**.

1. Sign in to PlayStation in your browser, open `ca.account.sony.com/api/v1/ssocookie`, copy all of the text it shows, paste it into **Login token** and press **Connect**. The token is used once to get access and is not stored; it is a login for your whole PlayStation account, so never share it or put it in a screenshot.
2. **Show my PS5 consoles**, then **Register on this Mac** next to your console. You also need to be signed in to PlayStation in OpenPS (the **Sign in** button at the top) and the app must have been built with `--psn-live` and have the key tables (see the README).
3. Wait while it works through the steps (Connecting, Asking your console to join, Waiting for your console, Reaching your console, Registering). It can take a minute or two. **Cancel** stops it at any time. The console must be on, or in rest mode with _Stay connected to the internet_, and signed in to the same account.
4. The console appears under **Consoles**. If you see "could not reach your console", the Mac and the console are probably on different networks or a firewall blocks UDP.

If it does not work, the log (`openps-data/logs/openps.log`) says which step failed. This feature has been tested against fakes built from the original's code and against byte-level references from the original, but **not yet against Sony's servers**: the first real run may reveal differences.

## 3. Play

Click **Connect** on a console card. A console in rest mode shows **Wake & connect**. The connection takes a few seconds; after 8 seconds the app tells you what to check. **Cancel** works at any moment.

While streaming, move the mouse to show the bars (not while keyboard and mouse control is on: then the mouse is game input and they only appear briefly when you switch control on or off). The **top bar** only shows information: console name, frame rate, bitrate, display rate, pacing. The pin at its end keeps it on screen while the controls fade away (remembered). The **bottom bar** is four tools on each side of the **PS button** (centre; press and hold works like the real button), mirrored about it. Left: **Games**, **Edit keys** and **Controls** (keyboard and mouse control on/off, F10). Right: **Stats** (hide or show the numbers in the top bar), **Sound** (mute), **Full screen** and **Leave** (disconnect, or put the console to rest), which sits in a pill of its own just right of the bar (above the bar, at its right edge, in a very narrow window), while the bar itself stays centred. A tool that is on is lit. Below 760 px wide the captions hide and the tiles shrink so nothing overflows. They hide after 3 seconds.

**Disconnect** asks what should happen to the console: _Disconnect_ (leave it on) or _Put the console in rest mode_. After a session the console card waits 10 seconds (the console is still closing the session), then looks for the console again and shows whether it is ready or in rest mode. The games row below uses the same lock.

### Games and trophies

When you are signed in to PlayStation Network, the Consoles page shows a trophy shelf (level, platinum/gold/silver/bronze, games, completed, average progress) and, under your consoles, **one row with the games installed on the console** (the console reports them to your account), each with its poster, trophy progress and size. Use the arrows or scroll sideways. The list and the posters are saved in the data folder (`cache/`), so they look the same on every start; **Update** refreshes them. They also refresh by themselves, quietly: a few seconds after a stream ends (what you played is new data) and whenever the list is 5 minutes old (only while OpenPS is in view and no stream is running).

- **Play** (the round button on a poster): does what **Connect** does (wakes the console if it is in rest mode, then connects), then starts that game on the console. Starting a game closes whichever one was running. The same lock as the Connect button applies: nothing starts while a session is open, while the console is waking, or during the short wait after a session.
- **Key map** (the drop-down under each game): picks which of your key-map profiles is used for that game. It is applied automatically when the game is started from the app. _Default key map_ leaves the profile as it is.
- **Games** (in the stream's bottom bar): the same list on top of the stream, to switch to another game without leaving. The console closes the current game and starts the one you pick.
- If the console says it is **still closing an earlier session**, the connect screen stays up ("Closing the previous session…") and reconnects by itself, for up to a minute and a half. **Cancel** stops it.
- The list needs the PlayStation Network connection (_Settings → PlayStation Network_). Games the console has installed but your account never played have no poster (a coloured tile is shown).

### Controller, keyboard and mouse

When a supported DualSense or DualShock 4 is connected, it controls the game by default. In the stream bar, **Controls** shows the current mode; click it or press **F10** to switch between the controller and keyboard and mouse. Switching modes releases the previous input source. The tile always says **Controls**; its picture is a controller, or a **mouse** while keyboard and mouse control is on, and a short message at the top of the stream says when it is switched on or off.

While a controller is connected, the Consoles page shows a small strip with its name, battery bar and percentage (with a bolt while it charges). The bar's fill follows the level and is red up to 25 %, yellow up to 50 % and green above. It disappears when the controller is disconnected.

Open **Settings → Controls** to see connected controllers, their type, slot and battery status, and adjust the stick deadzone. The deadzone ignores small movements around the stick centre and applies immediately.

**Rumble strength** (0–200 %, in steps of 10, default 100 %) scales the vibration a game asks for, and the Bluetooth haptics; above 100 % a game that asks for little vibration gets more, up to the controller's maximum. **Adaptive trigger strength** (0–100 %, default 100 %) is how hard the triggers push back: 100 % is the controller's full power (it cannot go higher), lower weakens every effect, and together with the console's own setting the weaker of the two applies. Each has a **−/+** pair, a **?** that explains it, and an icon **Test** button on its right: _rumble_ buzzes the controller for under a second, _trigger_ holds a resistance for 4 seconds so you can press the triggers and feel it. A Test button is disabled until its test has ended, and the controller is told to stop when it does. Both are DualSense settings and are saved with your other settings.

Console rumble and lightbar feedback are forwarded to DualSense controllers, with adaptive-trigger effects supported by the DualSense HID path. Bluetooth voice-coil haptics are streamed in the original app's format (the pad accepts every report; how they feel in games is still to be confirmed), and while they play the console's rumble is held back; USB PCM haptics are not played as voice-coil haptics because they use the controller's separate USB audio path; instead the haptic audio is turned into rumble on both motors (it follows the vibration setting, and stops within 150 ms of the audio ending).

**Vibration strength** follows the console's own setting (PS5 _Settings → Accessories → Controllers → Vibration Intensity_): Strong is full strength, Medium half, Weak about a third, Off none. Until the console reports a setting the controller vibrates at full strength. The adaptive-trigger setting is applied too: Medium and Weak soften the trigger effects, Off releases them.

**Several buttons at once.** The console applies only one new input event per packet, so when a change moves several controls together (for example LT and RT pressed in the same instant), each control is sent in a packet of its own, oldest first. This is what keeps a trigger from being missed or left held when two are pressed or released together.

When a controller is unplugged, loses focus, or you hand the console to the keyboard, its sticks, buttons and motion sensors are all reset, so the console never keeps seeing a stale tilt.

Keyboard and mouse mapping is **off** when a stream starts, so the mouse stays yours unless you switch to it. When enabled, the pointer is hidden and held in the middle of the window, the mouse drives the right stick, and no key or click reaches anything but the game. Mapping also stops by itself when the window loses focus.

**Key map names and games.** Names are lowercase snake_case (`night_drive`): whatever you type is converted when you save it (`Night Drive!` becomes `night_drive`), and a name that collides gets `_2`. Every key map has a permanent id, and a game remembers its key map by that id, so renaming a map never breaks a game's choice. If a map is deleted, the games that used it play with the default map, you get one note, and their tile shows "Key map removed, using default" until you pick another. **Add** in the key editor opens a list of the existing key maps (the one you are editing is highlighted); the new map starts as a copy of the one you pick. The shipped `racing` and `assassins_creed` maps are the ones from the installed copy of the app. Older saved choices (by name) are converted to ids automatically.

**Top bar fps.** With frame generation on it reads `30 → 60 fps` (what the game renders, then what is shown), next to the generation icon and its multiple; with it off it is just the game's fps.

**Always 60 fps.** There is no frame-rate setting: the console is always asked for 60 fps. New pictures are passed to the renderer as they arrive; at most one repeated picture is held as a fallback and displayed if no new picture arrives within 100/3 ms (the display thread checks every 50/3 ms). Where generated frames cannot be matched confidently, the nearer real frame is shown as it is (never a blend), so the picture stays crisp. Sharpness defaults to 60 %; saturation and sharpness apply even with upscaling and frame generation off. Settings saved before this keep working: a saved 30 fps becomes 60, and a sharpness still at the old default of 50 becomes 60.

**Frame rate and repeated pictures.** The console sends the stream's rate whatever the game renders, repeating pictures when the game is slower (a game at 30 on a 60 fps stream). The app compares pictures and counts only the different ones (the console re-encodes a repeated picture, so a copy is not identical: it counts as a repeat when it changed far less than the last few new pictures did; measured on a game at about 30 in a 60 fps stream, the copies were every other frame): that is the rate shown in the top bar, the rate frame generation multiplies (37 distinct pictures at 2× is 74), and it is measured from the frames as they arrive, not taken from the Settings value. A mostly still picture (a menu) keeps the last measured rate, and when a scene goes quiet (gameplay to a menu or an intro) no more than three pictures in a row are taken for repeats on the strength of the gameplay's larger changes, so the screen never freezes while the app re-learns what a repeat looks like there. The game's rate goes up at once (read from the last half second of new pictures) and comes down over a couple of seconds, so a game that was loading and starts to render again is not held at its low rate while real pictures arrive faster than they can be taken. Pictures are compared and counted as the frames arrive. Repeated pictures are not queued immediately; the renderer keeps at most one as a fallback, so a repeat cannot push a real picture out of the mailbox. The game's rate is the share of new pictures times the stream's rate (a burst of frames at the start cannot distort it). _Settings → Frame diagnostic → Record 30 s_ writes `logs/frame-diag.csv` (frame id, time, changed bytes, new picture) and shows how many frames arrived, how many were new pictures and how many ids were skipped.

### Your own key map

**Settings → Controls → Show in Finder** reveals `input.toml`. It is layered over the defaults, so you only write what differs:

```toml
[bindings]
CROSS = ["SPACE", "ENTER"]

[mouse]
sensitivity = 0.07

[profiles.shooter.bindings]
L2 = ["LEFT_SHIFT"]
```

Reserved keys (F7, F8, F10, F11, F12, ⌘) cannot be bound. A mistake in the file is reported with its line number and the default keys stay in use. **Reset to defaults** deletes the file. To start with mapping on, add `start_enabled = true`.

### Key maps that follow the scene (advanced mapping)

A game can need different keys on foot, in a vehicle and in a menu. In the key editor (**Edit keys**, F8):

- **Advanced mapping** (one switch for the key map you are editing; it can be changed during a stream) replaces the _Key_ and _Alternate_ columns with three: **On foot**, **In vehicle** and **In menu**, and it also turns on scene detection: a small vision model that reads the picture about twice a second while the keyboard and mouse are driving the console. There is no separate switch for the detector. The keys come from the column of the scene it sees: **on foot**, **in a vehicle**, or **in a menu** (menus _and_ cutscenes use the menu column); before it has seen anything, the on-foot column is used. The editor shows what it sees, for example `In a vehicle · first person · 91 %`, and the Controls tile names the scene. While the key map follows the scene, a small box in the top-right corner of the stream shows the key mode as an icon (feet for on foot, a car for in a vehicle, a menu for in a menu); it is always there, so nothing pops up when the scene changes. It is not shown while a controller drives the console or the scene model has not seen a picture yet, and a warning icon replaces it if the model cannot start. The first time the switch is turned on the three columns start as copies of the key column, so nothing stops working; turning it off brings the key and alternate columns back, as they were. Editing the keys and actions still needs mapping off.
- **Action** (a column of advanced key maps) gives a console button or trigger what it means in the game: _Enter or exit vehicle_ (`key_enter_or_exit_vehicle`), _Change camera_ (`key_change_camera`) or _Pause game_ (`key_pause_game`). A button has one action or none; an action can be on several buttons. Sticks and touchpad gestures cannot have one.
- **Layers in use** (advanced key maps; it can be changed during a stream): untick a layer the game has no use for, for example _In vehicle_ for a game without vehicles. Its keys stay in the file but are never used, the scene reader never picks that scene (its pictures count as the nearest scene you do use, so a vehicle-looking picture on foot cannot start the vehicle keys), and a key map in that scene keeps the keys of the first layer in use (on foot, then in a vehicle, then in a menu). Cutscenes use the _In menu_ layer. One layer always stays on. In the file: `disabled_layers = ["in_vehicle"]` in the key map's `advanced` table.

The actions make the guess steadier. _Enter or exit vehicle_ is what changes between on foot and in a vehicle, because the picture cannot tell a vehicle beside you from one you are in: a press out of a vehicle puts you on foot at once and, for twelve seconds, the pictures say nothing about on foot against in a vehicle; a press on foot moves half of the belief into the vehicle and the pictures then confirm or undo it. Starting to get in and then stopping (walking away, a press that entered nothing) leaves you on foot: the pictures of walking away undo the press within a few seconds, and the vehicle keys start once the pictures show you inside (`the_filter` tests `stopping_halfway_into_a_vehicle_leaves_you_on_foot` and `getting_in_is_confirmed_within_a_couple_of_seconds_of_sitting_down`). Without a press, pictures of a vehicle only slowly change on foot to in a vehicle (about ten seconds of nothing but vehicle pictures, longer after a long time on foot), while pictures that are clearly not of a vehicle take you out of one (knocked off a bike, a destroyed car). A menu or cutscene gives back the state it took. _Pause game_ does the same for menus (cutscenes start and end by themselves), and _Change camera_ for first and third person. An action that no button carries is not waited for: that change is then judged from the pictures alone. Measured on pictures the model had not seen, with simulated presses, this reads the scene right 87 % of the time (86 % before), on foot 84 % (70 %) and in a vehicle 93 % (98 %); on-foot pictures that look like a vehicle are read right 61 % of the time instead of 25 %.

In `input.toml` (per key map; the base map is the top-level `[advanced]`):

```toml
[profiles.gta_v.advanced]
enabled = true

[profiles.gta_v.advanced.on_foot]
CROSS = ["SPACE"]
TRIANGLE = ["F"]

[profiles.gta_v.advanced.in_vehicle]
CROSS = ["X"]
TRIANGLE = ["F"]

[profiles.gta_v.advanced.in_menu]
CROSS = ["RETURN"]

[profiles.gta_v.advanced.labels]
TRIANGLE = "key_enter_or_exit_vehicle"
TOUCHPAD = "key_change_camera"
OPTIONS = "key_pause_game"
```

**The model.** `assets/models/` holds two image models that Core ML runs, `scene-encoder.mlpackage` (MobileCLIP-S1, 41 MB) and `scene-backbone.mlpackage` (DINOv2-B, 164 MB), and `scene-probe.json`, the trained head that turns what they see into the four scenes and first/third person; the installer puts them in the app's `Resources/models`, and the first use compiles them into the data folder's `cache`. About twice a second a picture of the stream is shown to **both models whole** (squashed to their input size; the first version read only the middle square, which cut away the minimap and the rest of the HUD at the sides), which takes about 30 ms of Neural Engine time. The head was trained on about 3,300 labeled frames of GTA V and Watch Dogs gameplay, cutscenes and menus (`tools/scene-context/`, which also holds the scripts to relabel, retrain and evaluate, with `results/improve.txt` for what was tried). Measured on recordings it had not seen, a picture is read right 81 % of the time (73 % with the first version; 72 % against 62 % on Watch Dogs, 71 % against 9 % on first-person walking), and with the actions and the filter a new play session is read right about 90 % of the time (about 85 % before). It is still worse in a game it has not seen. Only presses made through the key map count as actions, and the scene only matters for the keyboard and mouse: while a physical controller drives the console (key map mapping off) the detector waits, using no CPU, and starts again when mapping is switched on; the switch itself stays as you set it.

**Teaching it a new game or more menus.** In `tools/scene-context/`: record a session with `scripts/record-session.sh` (up to 30 minutes, to `~/openps-captures`; the file holds the picture you streamed, keep it private), then run `./label.sh`. It extracts the new captures (one frame every 2 s) and serves the labeling page on this machine (`http://127.0.0.1:...`, opened in the browser): every recording is in it, labeled ones too, one set at a time (**N** / **P** or the list switch sets, each shown by a short id such as `9ad2` with the recording's name as a tooltip; the first entry, "all sets", lists every image at once, 500 more as you scroll, with the id on each card; the page opens on the first set with frames left, and **S** or **show** switches between the unlabeled frames and all frames, to revisit a finished set; `--new` leaves the labeled frames out). Label with 1–4 (on foot, in a vehicle of any kind, menu, cutscene) and F / T (view); 5 is _game_2d_ (a game that is not 3D) and 6 is _game_3d_ (a 3D game scene with some other in-game interaction: talking to someone, buying, ...); an older _in_game_ label means a game not yet told apart (**show** → in_game: 2D or 3D? lists them, to remap). The categories are menu, cutscene, on_foot, in_vehicle, game_3d and game_2d. game_2d and game_3d are kept in the dataset and sorted, and read by the classification tree (`./scene.sh tree`, see below), but the app's model does not read them yet, **Del** deletes a frame you do not need (Del again restores it while it is shown), double-click opens a large view, **size** changes the thumbnails and **show** hides what is labeled; for a vehicle you started to enter and then stopped, the label is _on foot_. Every change is saved at once into `dataset/labels.json` (the page says when it is saved), so nothing is lost if the tab closes; stop the page with Ctrl-C. Then run `./train.sh` (it computes features only for the new frames, trains, writes `assets/models`, and prints how recordings the model has not seen are read, with and without the filter), and `scripts/install.sh` puts the models in the app. The first run creates its own Python environment. The classification is meant to work as a tree: first game, cutscene or menu; of a game, 2D or 3D; of a 3D game, on foot, in a vehicle or another 3D scene (game_3d) (a cutscene, a menu and a 2D game are not looked at further). `./scene.sh tree` reads and scores it that way, with one small head per question, against a single flat head over the six scenes, on recordings neither has seen; the app still reads the four scenes (on foot, in a vehicle, menu, cutscene). A folder of screenshots and clips goes in with `./scene.sh ingest FOLDER` (recordings `shots-NNN` and `clips-NNN`, one per long clip), and `./label.sh --only shots-001` opens just some of the recordings (by the start of a name or of an id). `./scene.sh suggest` picks the unlabeled frames worth labeling next (the ones the model is least sure about or that look new to it, 500 by default) and `./label.sh --suggested` shows them as one set, best first. `./train.sh --reexport` converts the models to Core ML again (they are converted only when they are missing or what they see changed); `./scene.sh --help` lists the single steps (`extract`, `ingest`, `tree`, `suggest`, `label`, `import`, `sort`, `status`, `train`, `eval`, `check`, `fixtures`). The labeled dataset is `tools/scene-context/dataset/`, kept in git: one `labels.json` for every label (and the frames you deleted), one `recordings.json` with what the tools need to know of each recording (its order, whether it is a continuous sequence, how it is thinned) and one `frames/` folder with the labeled 480 px frames (`<id>__f_NNNNN.jpg`), so a fresh clone can train; a recording is identified everywhere by a 4-character id (the start of the SHA-1 of its name), the names stay on this Mac in `data/index.json`; it is also sorted into `dataset/sorted/` (first_person, third_person, on_foot, in_vehicle, in_menu, in_game, game_2d, game_3d; generated, not in git). The captures, every other extracted frame (`data/frames` and its `index.json`), the feature cache and the trained models stay on this Mac.

## 4. Settings

Stream quality (applies at the next connect):

- **Sharpness** (a percentage, 0–200, default 60): with upscaling on, 0 is a plain smooth enlargement, 100 is the GPU upscaler as it draws it (it adds edge contrast the real picture does not have) and more than 100 is sharper still. With upscaling off, 50 leaves the picture alone, more sharpens and less softens.
- **Adaptive sharpening** (a percentage, 0–200, default 100; needs upscaling): AMD's _RCAS_, a contrast-adaptive sharpening that puts back the crisp edges the sharpness blend takes out and leaves flat areas alone. It is its own slider, independent of Sharpness: 0 is off, 100 % is the strength that measured closest to the real picture with MetalFX at sharpness 60 %, 200 % is twice that lobe. With the MetalFX engine the scaler also runs in **linear light**. Measured on real footage against the truth (`tasks/enhance-compare/`), sharpness 60 % + RCAS 100 % is closer to the real picture than the blend alone at every bitrate (about 1 to 4 % on a stream, 22 % on clean input); with the **FSR** engine it is the sharpening FSR is designed to be followed by. Cost: about 0.4 ms of GPU time per picture at 1440p and about 2 ms at 4K (an M4).
  - **The finishing-pass guard** (what keeps this from costing frames when **Adaptive frame rate** is off; with it on, the frame rate is lowered instead, see below). The pass has a fixed cost per displayed picture, small at 60 Hz and a lot at 120 Hz in 4K together with frame generation. The renderer measures the GPU time of every displayed picture; a picture counts as _too busy_ when the GPU needed more than 70 % of the time between two displayed pictures (5.8 ms at 120 Hz, 11.7 ms at 60 Hz). After **120 too-busy pictures in a row** (one to two seconds) it switches **linear light and adaptive sharpening off** and uses the plain upscaler (one slow picture does not count, and the count starts over at the first quick one). It stays off until you change the upscaling, sharpness, adaptive sharpening or frame-generation settings (then it tries again), and it logs one line when it trips. It only ever takes away the optional finishing; the upscaling and frame generation keep running. The Debug bar shows its state live.

- **Saturation** (a percentage, 0–200, default 100): 100 is the picture as the console sends it, more is more vivid, less is paler.
- **Frame generation** (a switch; **there is no rate to choose**): _Off_ shows frames as they arrive (up to 60 per second). _On_ makes frames here to **fill your display's refresh rate** (up to 240), because the console never sends more than 60: on a 144 Hz display a 60 fps stream is shown at 144, on a 120 Hz one at 120, on a 60 Hz one nothing is generated. It does not have to divide the refresh rate evenly (see _Pacing and the display_ below). The overlay shows the rate being presented. Settings saved with the old multiples (1.5× to 3×), a target rate or a display rate become this switch when they are loaded (on if frame generation was on, off otherwise); the rate they carried is ignored.
- **Adaptive frame rate** (a switch, on by default): this is what keeps frame generation automatic. When this Mac cannot keep **every filter** you have switched on running at the display's rate, the frame rate comes down in **steps of 5 fps** and the picture stays exactly as you set it: Macs with room to spare show the full rate and weaker ones show the same picture more slowly, instead of a filter being switched off. **It moves gently, and not on a blip.** The renderer measures the GPU time of every picture over the last second: the **mean** (how much of the time the GPU is busy; too high above **75 %**) and the **slowest 5 %** (a picture that takes longer than the time between two refreshes is shown late, which is a spike in the frame time however idle the GPU is on average; too high above **100 %** of that time). A real picture costs more than a generated one (about 5.5 ms against 3.3 ms at 1080p to 1620p on an M4, with the stream at 60 and the display at 144), so it is the mix over a second that counts. The rate comes down only after the load has been too high for **2 seconds**, by **at most 10 fps** at a time and at least **1.5 seconds apart**, each step toward the rate the measured times fit in: a short spike takes nothing, a big overload settles in a few seconds with no lurch. It goes **up one step of 5** only when the next step would still use under **60 %** of the time (the slowest 5 % under 90 %) for **5 seconds**, and never within **10 seconds** of coming down, so it does not saw back and forth. The wide gap between the limits is what lets a steady load settle on one step and stay there (tests with steady, noisy and spiky loads pin that). Changing a filter, the upscaling, the engine, the sharpness or the switch itself forgets the limit and tries the full rate again. (Before, every picture was judged alone and one light picture cancelled the count of heavy ones, so a Mac whose real pictures were too slow while its generated ones were quick was never slowed down and showed late frames; and a step down jumped straight to the new rate.) When fewer pictures are shown than the stream sends (a held rate below 60), the real pictures in between are dropped evenly, each shown one when it is due. **On a display with a fixed refresh rate** (not variable refresh) the held rate is also rounded down to one that shows every picture for the **same whole number of refreshes** (60 Hz: 60, 30, 20, 15; 144 Hz: 144, 72, 48, 36, 29): 45 on a 60 Hz screen would show pictures for one refresh and then two, alternating, and measured ±8 ms of pacing error with a 37 ms longest gap, against ±0.6 ms at 30. A rate **within a tenth of the next even rate up** is kept as it is (55 on 60 Hz doubles one picture in eleven; 50 does not qualify and becomes 30), so a Mac that can deliver 55 is not held to 30. The chip and the Debug bar show the rate that is really held. Variable-refresh displays keep the plain steps of 5. If the display's refresh rate cannot be read, "the display's rate" is taken as at most 120 Hz instead of 240. The stream bar shows an amber chip with the held rate, the Debug bar says "holding 45 fps (144 asked for)" (the display's rate is what is asked for), and the log has one line each time the limit changes. With the switch **off** the rate is kept and the finishing-pass guard above gives up linear light and adaptive sharpening instead.
- **The top bar numbers** (the **Stats** button): each item has a width of its own, so a number gaining or losing a digit does not move the others. **fps · avg**: the game's own frame rate now, and its average over the last 10 seconds (what frame generation adds is not in it). The **sparkle** item (only while frame generation is on): the frame rate that is shown on screen, with the multiple in its tooltip. **ms · avg**: the frame time, the time between two frames on the screen, now and averaged over the last 10 seconds. **±ms**: the _frame-to-frame deviation_: how much the time between two frames on the screen varies; a smooth picture is under 1 ms, and the tooltip has the longest gap and the late frames of the last two seconds. The amber item is the adaptive frame rate's held rate; **cam ms** appears when camera compensation is on.
- **Shimmering lines in a camera pan** (a radiator, railings, a fence seen while the camera turns). Frame generation finds where each block of the picture moved to by comparing the two real frames, and a row of identical lines fits the next line over as well as the right one, so the match used to jump from line to line, block to block and frame to frame. It now (1) prefers the shorter vector and the one nearest to what the block did a frame ago (a camera does not jump a whole line spacing between two frames), (2) uses the motion of the picture as a whole, found on a quarter-size copy, when something else in the picture makes it unambiguous (so a fast pan outside the old 16 px search range is found), (3) refines the motion to quarter pixels instead of whole pairs of pixels, and (4) resamples with a sharp (Catmull-Rom) filter instead of a bilinear one, which softened one-pixel lines in the generated frame and not in the real ones. A recording of a real session (a radiator in a hallway, HEVC 1080p 60 fps, 29 s) showed what the first version missed: inside the fins no block is clear (the fins repeat every 12 pixels and move 5), the blocks flipped between the right vector and the one a spacing away (5 and −7 pixels), and a patch of them showed the fins in the wrong place. So (5) every block now says whether its best match is clearly better than any look-alike far from it, and ambiguous blocks adopt the vector of the clear blocks next to them (the edges of the radiator), over eight rounds so that it spreads across the pattern, if that vector fits them nearly as well as their own, and (6) the vector of a fine block is pulled toward the median of its neighbourhood, and the neighbours' vectors are only tried where the block's own two samples disagree, judged over a small window instead of one pixel. Replayed (every second picture of the capture fed in, the one between generated and compared with the real one): error on thin vertical structure 11.5 → 10.2 levels (the nearer real picture: 16.1) and the share of those pixels more than 24 levels off 16.3 % → 13.6 % (25.4 %); on the radiator itself 4.5 → 2.8 levels, and the patch is gone. What cannot be told from two frames alone remains: if the lines move by more than half their spacing between two frames and nothing else in the picture says how far, there is no right answer and the frame is wrong either way.
- **Shimmer that grows with the generated frame rate: generated pictures were softer than real ones.** Checked with a replay that makes the pictures a display would show in a row (60 fps stream, 144 output ticks a second, each at its own position between two real pictures) and measures their detail (mean absolute Laplacian) against the real pictures': generated pictures had **88 to 93 %** of the detail of the real ones (and **91 %** of the grain), and the detail changed by 5.9 % from one picture to the next against 1.6 % between real pictures. Two causes: a generated picture was the **average of the two real pictures** (which loses detail wherever they are a hair out of line, and halves the grain: two independent grains average to 0.71 of either), and the bilinear/Catmull-Rom resampling of a picture moved by a fraction of a pixel softens it. The real pictures are full strength, so the detail pulsed at the beat of the generated frames, and the more of them there were the more of the time it was soft. Now a generated picture is taken from the **nearer real picture alone**, moved to where the camera has taken it (no blend), with a slightly sharper cubic filter (Keys, a = −0.75): detail **97.6 to 103 % of the real pictures'** at 144 and 99.6 to 105 % at 240, change from picture to picture 3.75 % (144) and 4.3 % (240) instead of 5.9 %. It costs some accuracy against the exact picture on a still-noisy recording (mean error 12.1 → 13.2 levels) because the noise of a single picture is not averaged away: the picture is as sharp and as grainy as the real ones, and that is what keeps it from shimmering. Where no motion explains a pixel (an occlusion) the two real pictures are still blended, as before.
- **Are the motion vectors right when the camera moves or you move forward?** Checked against exact ground truth (`tests/camera_motion.rs`: a continuous scene, so any picture between two frames can be drawn exactly): a pan of 2, 7 and 14 px a frame, **moving forward** (the picture grows from the middle by 0.5, 1 and 2 % a frame: vectors that grow with the distance from it), a **roll** of 0.3 and 0.8 degrees a frame, and a pan over two layers (the near one twice as fast). The motion field is within **0.0 to 1.0 px** of the exact vectors on average (1 % of blocks more than 2 px off at worst), and the generated picture is within **0.0 to 2.9 levels** of the exact one against 2 to 67 for the nearer real picture. So the vectors and the units (half-size pixels, quarter-pixel refinement, ×2 to the picture, the sign convention of MetalFX's motion) are right on a textured scene; what goes wrong in real play is flat, repeating, dark or occluded content, handled by the checks above. The upscalers were checked the same way: MetalFX against a faithful enlargement, linear light against perceptual (0.73 levels apart on smooth content), and the FSR kernel against a translation of AMD's reference (within 0.6 of a level).
- **Shimmer when the camera turns side to side: using the controller for the motion vectors.** The picture alone can say the wrong thing about a pan: lines that move by nearly a whole spacing look almost the same in both frames (a 19 px flick over fins 22 px apart looks like a 3 px step back), and a plain wall has nothing to match at all. The app knows what the stick asked for (`SharedLook` holds what was sent to the console, from a pad or from the keyboard/mouse mapper's right stick, so it is device-independent; the keyboard/mouse stick is recorded only while it is the one reaching the console, and `mouse.look = LEFT_STICK` is not seen) and learns how the game answers it: the delay (lag) and the picture pixels per unit of stick, by comparing the stick history with the motion measured on the picture (needs about 40 measurements, the stick explaining at least 60 % of the measured motion, and an identifiable lag; it never fires on a still scene). The motion the stick commanded between two real frames is then handed to the motion search (`Gpu::set_input_prior`) as one more place to look, with the motion of the picture as a whole and the last frame's vector, and it wins among matches that are equally good (a small cost by distance from it, larger the less it is trusted); blocks no neighbour can settle take it as well when there is no consensus in the picture. The learning runs whenever frame generation does (it no longer needs _Camera compensation_, which is a separate, off-by-default setting). **Debug tab → Input motion prior** says whether it is in use. Checked on a synthetic plain wall with a radiator (`tests/periodic.rs`): a flick of 19 px over fins 22 px apart is 85 levels off with the picture alone and 1.6 with the stick (1.6 also when the stick's guess is 5 % off), 11 px over 14 px: 85 → 1.7; a prior that is wrong or says "still" does not spoil a pan the picture can tell (1.6 to 2.3 levels against 1.6). Limits: a flick of exactly one whole spacing (identical pictures) improves (83 → 20) but is not exact; the prior cannot be replayed on the recording (it holds pictures, not the stick), so it has not been measured on a real game, and a game whose camera does not follow the stick in a learnable way (an acceleration curve, a dead zone that changes) leaves it off.
- **Long gaps (20 to 30 ms) in frame pacing, and the Smoothing buffer** (Settings → Display; on by default). In Smooth mode the picture shows the space between two real frames, and the next real frame has to be there when that space starts: the content clock is set to the best case the network has shown, so a frame that arrives even a little later has nothing to interpolate toward and the picture **holds** for as long as it is late, then jumps. A console does not deliver frames evenly: on a recording of a real session (60 fps, wired) the pictures arrived up to 18 to 30 ms apart now and then. Replayed on those arrival times at 144 Hz, Smooth mode **without** the buffer held the picture 388 times in 29 seconds (12 of them 20 ms or more, 12 % of the time); **with** it, 18 times, the longest 6.9 ms (0.4 % of the time). The buffer holds the picture back by how late the slowest 2 % of the last three seconds of frames were (at most 25 ms; it was 17 ms on that recording), changing by at most 0.4 ms a frame so that it is never seen. **It costs that much latency, in Smooth mode only**; Low latency mode (which predicts instead of waiting) never uses it. If you would rather have the lowest delay and put up with the holds, turn it off; the Debug bar's _Smoothing buffer_ row says what it is doing.
- **Run above the display's refresh rate** (Settings → Display; off by default; needs frame generation): for a monitor with a low refresh rate. Normally the renderer never makes more frames than the display can show. With this on, frames are generated and handed over at up to 240 a second on any display. A 60 Hz screen still shows 60 pictures a second, so it does not look smoother, but **each picture is the newest one made rather than one made earlier**, which lowers the delay between the console's frame and the glass, and the older ones are dropped. It costs a lot of GPU (every extra frame is drawn, upscaled and handed over), so the adaptive frame rate will lower it gently when the Mac cannot keep up, and nothing is held back that the Mac can do. **Windowed only**: in full screen the frames would only queue up (and add delay), so the display's rate is kept there; the Debug bar's _Above the display's rate_ row says whether it is in use. Which of the two to expect is in the hint under the switch.
- **Locked rate:** the rate is the display's (or what Adaptive frame rate holds it to). The frame-interval numbers come from when Metal says each frame reached the screen.
- **Pacing and the display:** if the display has variable refresh switched on (System Settings → Displays; the HUD shows "VRR"), frames are scheduled for their moment and the display follows. If it does not, a rate that does not divide the refresh rate (120 on a 144 Hz panel) is paced by the app's own clock instead of the display's: the spacing is even and a frame may tear. Rates that divide the refresh rate are held for whole refreshes and never tear.
- **Engine** (Settings → Display, on Macs that have the GPU renderer): who **upscales** the picture: Apple's _MetalFX_ (default) or _FSR_ (AMD's FidelityFX Super Resolution 1, see below). **MetalFX's frame generation makes the in-between frames with either engine**: in Smooth mode at about **2×** (60 to 120), where every generated frame is the midpoint between two real ones, MetalFX makes that midpoint and the chosen engine upscales it. Our own interpolator makes every other generated frame, and the frame whenever MetalFX cannot (still starting, too slow). MetalFX only makes exact midpoints, and mixing them with our own frames at other rates made the picture flicker, so those rates (for example 60 to 144) use our interpolator alone. Earlier builds had separate choices for the upscaler and for frame generation, and a VideoToolbox engine; a saved VideoToolbox choice now opens as MetalFX.
- **Resolution** 360p, 540p, 720p, 1080p. **Bitrate**: _Auto_ asks the console for as much as your connection can carry, **up to 100 Mbit/s** (the most the original accepts; a real PS5 ended the stream seconds after the start when asked for 800). It looks at the link per connection (wired link speed, or the Wi-Fi rate, and only for a console on the same network; a console behind a router gets the normal request). The console decides what it really sends. If it refuses the request, or ends the stream within 20 seconds of the start, OpenPS connects again with a normal value and does not raise it for that console again until it is restarted (see `openps-data/logs/openps.log`). Or pick 2/6/10/15 Mbit/s.
- **Codec** HEVC (better quality per bit, used when this Mac can decode it) or H.264. PS4 always streams H.264.
- **HDR** needs HEVC and an HDR display; PS5 only.

Display (applies immediately, also while streaming):

- **Volume**, and mute with the HUD button.
- **Upscaling** (MetalFX or FSR, see **Engine**): _Off_, _1.33×_, _1.5×_ or _2×_. The picture is made that many times larger than the stream's resolution, **whatever the window or screen is** (1080p at 2× is made at 4K even on a 1440p screen) and then scaled down to fit the window, which usually looks better than upscaling only to the window's size; the small badge on each option is the size it gives for the resolution chosen above (1080p: 1440p, 1620p, 2160p; 720p: 960p, 1080p, 1440p). During a stream the top bar shows the upscale icon with the height now in use. _Off_ really is off. Costs about 1.3 to 2.3 ms of GPU time per frame (more the larger the picture is made). Settings saved with the old _Up to 1440p_ / _Up to 4K_ become the nearest multiple of your resolution.
- **Immersion** (off by default; Settings → Display, or the **Immersion** button in the stream bar while streaming): when the window is wider or taller than the picture, the black bars are filled with one soft glow of the picture's colours: the whole picture, shrunk, stretched over the window sideways or upwards as the window needs and blurred, like the ambient light behind a video (no repeated or mirrored copies). It needs the Metal renderer and does nothing when there are no bars.
- **Every filter has its own switch** (Settings → Display), and each switch keeps its slider's value, so a filter can be turned off and back on: **Linear light** (MetalFX engine only), **Sharpness**, **Adaptive sharpening**, **Saturation** and **Frame generation**, next to the existing Upscaling (Off), Camera latency compensation and Immersion. A switched-off filter leaves the picture as it was: no blend with a plain enlargement (the upscaler's picture as it draws it, or no unsharp mask without upscaling), no RCAS, saturation 100 %, frames as they arrive. The Debug bar says "off by choice" for each.
- **Frame generation** and **Adaptive frame rate**: see above.
- **Frame Gen Latency**: **Smooth** (the default) interpolates between frames: the cleanest picture, at the cost of **one source frame (about 17 ms at 60 fps) of latency**, which stays low enough to feel responsive. **Low latency** predicts forward from the last frame and adds no delay but can show small artifacts around fast edges. Measured on real footage, interpolation clearly reduces error and extrapolation only marginally beats repeating a frame; see `analysis/spec/06-frame-generation-research.md`.
- **FSR** is the second engine, for upscaling: AMD's FidelityFX Super Resolution 1, **EASU** (edge-adaptive spatial upsampling), ported line by line from AMD's reference (`ffx_fsr1.h`, MIT licence, Copyright (c) 2021 Advanced Micro Devices, Inc.; the notice is in `crates/openps-metal/src/shaders.rs`) to Metal compute shaders. It works on the finished picture, followed by the same blend and **RCAS** sharpening as the MetalFX picture gets (RCAS is the other half of FSR 1). It runs on every Mac that has the GPU renderer, needs no macOS version, and has no linear-light mode (FSR 1 is made for the picture as it is). The kernel agrees with a straight translation of AMD's reference to within 0.8 of an 8-bit level on every test picture (a test in `crates/openps-metal/tests/gpu.rs` pins that). FSR only upscales: frame generation is MetalFX's at about 2× and our own interpolator's at other rates, as with the MetalFX engine (FSR 3's frame generation needs depth and game motion vectors, see below).
  - **Why not FSR 3 and FSR 4?** They cannot be used on a video stream as they are, which is why FSR 1 is what is offered. AMD's FSR SDK (version 2.4.0 at the time of writing) has backends for DirectX 12 and Vulkan only, none for Metal. **FSR 3** (its temporal upscaler and its frame generation, optical flow plus 11 interpolation passes, 18 HLSL compute passes in all, MIT-licensed source) takes what a game engine hands it every frame: a **depth buffer, motion vectors and the camera's sub-pixel jitter**. A remote-play stream carries none of them (it is a finished, already-compressed picture); they would have to be invented, and measured with Apple's temporal scaler on real footage, a temporal upscaler fed with estimated motion made the picture worse (`tasks/enhance-compare/`). Porting its frame generation to Metal is possible in principle but is a large job whose output could not be checked against AMD's (there is no Mac build to compare with), so the **built-in interpolator makes the generated frames with either engine**. **FSR 4** is a machine-learning model. A third-party Metal port exists ([UzenUPoZiTiV4ik/fsr4-native-macos](https://github.com/UzenUPoZiTiV4ik/fsr4-native-macos): FSR 4.1 INT8 on Apple Silicon, 2.8 ms at 720p→1080p on an M5 and about 4.8 ms with its M1 to M4 path, measured by its author on an M5 only), but it is built for a game's inputs too (colour, depth, motion vectors, jitter, exposure; its standalone runner takes recorded frames of those), needs macOS 26 and Xcode's Metal toolchain to build, and its default 4.1.2 Lite weights are read from an AMD **driver** DLL whose licence was not checked (the 4.1.1 weights come from an SDK DLL that AMD's licence file lists under the MIT licence). It is not integrated. The part of FSR that is a published algorithm and takes a finished picture is FSR 1, and that is what is here.
- **Debug tools** (off by default; Settings → Display): adds an **Engine** button at the left end of the stream bar. It opens a small panel to switch between _MetalFX_ and _FSR_ **on the running stream**, a **Resolution** row (Off / 1.33× / 1.5× / 2×, with the size each gives) that changes the upscaling on the running stream, and a line that says what is really doing the upscaling and the generated frames right now (for example "upscaling FSR to 1440p (adaptive sharpening); generated frames our interpolator"), and the held frame rate when Adaptive frame rate is holding one. It changes the same saved settings as the Settings page. Use it to compare the engines on the same scene. It also shows the **Debug bar**, a panel at the left of the stream (fold it with the minus button, copy everything as text with the copy button) that lists everything running in the backend, each line with a dot: green = on, grey = off by choice, amber = degraded or a guard has tripped, red = failing, hollow = not available on this Mac. Sections: _Running_ (renderer, upscaling and by which engine, finishing pass, linear light, sharpness, adaptive sharpening, saturation, frame generation and by which engine, camera compensation, immersion, the engine chosen), _This Mac_ (what exists: Metal, MetalFX, FSR, the display), _Safety guards_ (the adaptive frame rate with its counter and any held rate, and the finishing-pass guard), _Timing and GPU_ (the game's rate, shown rate, pacing, GPU time per picture and in flight, latency, frame counts and drops, GPU errors), _Stream_ (what was asked for, bitrate received, packets and drops), _Sound and controls_ (audio problems and underruns, the controller and its battery, keys and mouse, rumble and trigger strength). It shows the settings the renderer is actually running with, so a choice that did not take effect is visible.
- **Testing the adaptive frame rate** without a slow Mac: start the app with `OPENPS_EXTRA_GPU_MS=14` (milliseconds added to every picture's measured GPU time, 0 to 1000, nothing is drawn differently) and the rate steps down as if the filters cost that much more.
- **Camera latency compensation** (on by default): turns the picture immediately when you move the mouse, before the console answers. It learns how your game responds and switches itself off when it cannot tell. Not validated against a real game.

Try **Refresh**, or add it by IP address.                                                                                                                                                            |

Rest mode works if _Enable turning on PS5 from network_ is on; use **Wake & connect**.                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |

The app sends exactly what the original does; see `crates/openps-rp/tests/oracle/README.md`. |
| "registration rejected … code 80108b02"           | The PSN account is not one that is signed in on the console, or the code was wrong. | Stuck on "Connecting"                             | Wait for the hint at 8 s, check power and network, **Cancel** and retry. | Artifacts or stutter                              | Lower the resolution or bitrate; use Ethernet; set _Frame generation_ to Off and _Frame Gen Latency_ to Smooth to compare.                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |

Logs: `openps-data/logs/openps.log` (inside the app). They hold no keys, PINs or tokens. Nothing is sent anywhere.

### Controller input diagnostics

If a physical controller seems to send the wrong thing, start the app from a terminal with tracing on (a double-click launch cannot pass the variable):

```sh
OPENPS_INPUT_TRACE=1 ./install/OpenPS.app/Contents/MacOS/openps-app
```

Every pad change is then logged with a microsecond clock (`t=`) at each stage: `source_emit` (what macOS reported), `arbiter` (stored state, and whether it was forwarded), `feedback_encode`, `wire_protect` (one line per history packet: its sequence, key position and records; a change that moves several controls at once is sent as one packet per control, because the console applies only one new event per packet; trigger records are `86` for LT and `87` for RT, last byte the value) and `udp_send` (whether the socket accepted that same packet). While tracing, the log file is not rotated, so it grows; delete it afterwards. Packets the socket refuses for transient reasons are logged as warnings even without tracing. A `udp_send` line proves the packet left the Mac, not that the console applied it.

### Video capture for bug reports

To study picture problems (corruption after packet loss, a black screen), start the app from a terminal with a capture directory:

```sh
OPENPS_VIDEO_CAPTURE="$PWD/captures" ./install/OpenPS.app/Contents/MacOS/openps-app
```

Each session then writes `video-<time>.opsvid` there: every video frame the console sent, with its frame id and arrival time, and the codec parameter sets. It stops by itself after 90 seconds or 200 MB (about 30 seconds at 50 Mbit/s), or after 30 minutes or 4 GB when started with `scripts/record-session.sh`; `OPENPS_VIDEO_CAPTURE_SECONDS` and `OPENPS_VIDEO_CAPTURE_MB` raise those limits for a long recording (the whole file is read into memory when analysed). A gap in the frame ids is a loss, and the log notes it as `video loss`. **The file contains the picture you were streaming**, so keep it to yourself. Analyse it with `OPENPS_CAPTURE_FILE=<file> cargo test -p openps-video --release --test capture_analysis -- --ignored --nocapture`. It is off by default. **To record a session for a picture problem** (for example shimmering in a camera pan), run `scripts/record-session.sh`: it starts the installed app with the capture on, writing to `~/openps-captures`. Replay it through frame generation with `OPENPS_CAPTURE_FILE=<file> OPENPS_REPLAY_OUT=<dir> cargo test -p openps-metal --release --test replay -- --ignored --nocapture`: every second picture is fed in and the one in between is generated and compared with the real one, with the error measured on thin vertical structure and the worst pair written out as PPM files (`OPENPS_REPLAY_FX=1` makes the midpoints with MetalFX instead).

## 6. Uninstall

`scripts/uninstall.sh [--dest DIR] [--keep-data]`, or just delete `OpenPS.app`. All data is inside it.
